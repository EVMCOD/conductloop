# Outreach Sequence

Cold outreach as a small experiment, not a campaign. One segment, one message, a fixed number of touches, and a number that tells you the
message is wrong.

## When to use

- A new segment has to be opened and there is no inbound yet.
- Reply rate has fallen and the message needs a diagnosis.
- A specific event (a launch, a pricing change, a new market) makes a list of accounts worth contacting.

Not for: accounts that already replied or asked for a demo (those are pipeline), and not for blanket blasts to a purchased list.

## Step 1 - Pick one segment

A segment is: the same role, the same problem, the same trigger, in a company size band you can serve.

- One segment per sequence, 25 to 50 named accounts. More than 50 named accounts is not outreach, it is spam.
- Every account must match on role title, company size band, the observable trigger, and one disqualifier you checked.
- Name the trigger in one line before writing: "teams that just hired a Head of Ops and are still running the process in spreadsheets".
- Write 3 disqualifiers. An account that fails one is off the list, and the disqualifier is recorded so the next list is better.
- Rank the list by fit, not alphabetically. Work the top half first and leave the rest for round 2.

If you cannot name the trigger in one sentence, you do not have a segment yet, and a message built on it will fail.

## Step 2 - Write the message

Structure, in this order, 60 to 90 words total:

1. **Line 1: the hook.** The specific, observable thing about them (their trigger), not a compliment. "Saw the ops team you posted about
   onboarding time" beats "I love what you are building".
2. **Line 2: the problem.** One sentence naming the cost of the status quo in their terms. No features.
3. **Line 3: the proof.** One line of evidence that this works: a result, a number, a customer of similar size, or a specific observation.
   No logo wall.
4. **Line 4: the ask.** One low-friction action with a deadline: "worth 15 minutes next Tuesday to see if this is worth a pilot?" Not "let
   me know if you want to chat".

Rules:

- Under 90 words, 4 to 6 sentences. The subject line is under 6 words and must survive truncation: no "Re:", no "Quick question", no fake
  personalisation punctuation.
- No attachments, no links beyond one, no calendar link in the first message, one idea per message.
- Plain text, one paragraph or a subject plus one short body. It will be read on a phone between meetings.
- Name the outcome you sell, not the category. "Close the month in 2 hours instead of 2 days" beats "finance automation".
- No false familiarity, no "I hope this email finds you well", no "quick question" when the message is a pitch.

Length rule: over 120 words and it is a letter, and letters do not get replies. Under 40 words and it is usually missing the problem or the
proof.

## Step 3 - Run a fixed cadence

Four touches maximum, 14 days, each with a reason to send it:

- **Day 0**: the message above.
- **Day 3**: one new piece of information (a result, a relevant change, a short observation about their situation). No "just bumping this".
- **Day 8**: the ask again, shortened to two sentences, with a reason this time ("closing the quarter, so this is the last time I will raise
  it").
- **Day 14**: a close-out that gives permission: "I will stop here. If this is a priority next quarter, reply and I will pick it up in May."

Rules:

- No touch after a reply, in any direction. A reply moves the account to pipeline or to the do-not-contact list.
- Never more than 2 touches in one day to the same company, and one contact per company unless asked otherwise.
- No attachments ever in the sequence; a link to one page is the maximum.
- Send the first 10 accounts of a new segment manually. Automation after the manual sample, not before.

## Step 4 - Read the numbers and know when the message is wrong

Measure per sequence, not per message: sends, replies, positive replies, meetings held, and meetings that happened.

| Positive reply rate (replies / sends) | Verdict | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Under 1% after 50+ sends | the message is wrong, or the list is | rewrite lines 1 and 2, test on a fresh list of 50 |
| 1% to 3% | the segment works, the message is flat | keep the list, rewrite only the ask (line 4) |
| 3% to 6% | working | keep sending, then test one variable (proof or hook) |
| Over 6% | strong | raise the daily volume, do not change the message yet |

- Judge a message after at least 50 sends. Fewer tells you nothing, and changing it before 50 sends is noise-chasing.
- Change one variable per test. Hook, problem, proof, ask: one at a time.
- A reply that is not positive still counts as a reply for message quality; count positive replies separately for segment quality.
- 5 positive replies with 0 meetings means the ask is bad (usually too big), not that the segment is bad.
- Track the objection in the reply. 3 replies with the same objection is a segment or positioning problem, and it goes to the product memo,
  not the next message.

## Step 5 - Stop rules

Stop a sequence when the account replies (move to pipeline or suppression), asks to be removed (suppress permanently and note it), bounces
twice (mark undeliverable, do not try a variant address), or has had 4 touches with no reply (stop for 6 months, then only re-approach with
a genuinely new trigger). A sequence that reaches 50 sends with under 1% replies: kill the message, keep the segment, and write the reason
in the log.

## What a good answer looks like

- The segment definition in one line, plus the 3 disqualifiers.
- The message, under 90 words, with the four parts visible.
- A cadence table with dates and the reason for each touch.
- Numbers after at least 50 sends, with the verdict band and the next single variable to test.
- A suppression list and a written reason for every stop.

## Common traps

- Personalising line 1 so hard it sounds like a template got mangled.
- Sending 5 follow-ups and calling it persistence.
- "Just bumping this" as a touch; it burns the sender reputation for that domain and helps nobody.
- Measuring opens instead of replies. Opens are noise; replies are the signal.
- Changing the message after 10 sends, then changing it again after 15.
- Mailing 500 accounts from a list with no disqualifiers.
- Asking a stranger for a 30-minute demo in the first message.
- Treating a polite decline as a reply-rate failure when the objection repeats.
