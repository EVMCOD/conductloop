# Contract Review

A review pass that produces a decision per clause, a short list of escalations, and one recommendation. It is a triage of risk, not a substitute for a
qualified lawyer in the local jurisdiction.

## When to use

- A standard agreement comes back with edits, from a customer, partner, agency or supplier.
- A deal is signed without review, or the terms differ from the template.
- A counterparty asks for exclusivity, a custom liability cap, or source code escrow.
- A contract auto-renews and nobody read the renewal clause.

Not for: drafting from scratch (use a template), or a routine NDA with a known counterparty type and no edits.

## Step 1 - Classify the document and its leverage

Write these down before reading the body:

- **Type**: customer order form, MSA, NDA, DPA, partner or reseller agreement, contractor agreement, software licence, mutual NDA.
- **Who drafted it**: our template, theirs, or negotiated.
- **Leverage**: are we the only realistic supplier, or one of many? At the start of a customer relationship, leverage is usually theirs.
- **Value**: the total contract value and the term length, both as numbers.
- **Reversibility**: can the founder walk away from this deal next month at low cost? A Yes means negotiate on price and speed, not on risk.

Do not read a 20-page MSA without these five answers written down. Every clause matters differently depending on them.

## Step 2 - The clauses that decide the deal

Check these, in this order. For each, write the counterparty position, the standard position, and the deviation.

1. **Liability cap**: our standard is total fees paid in the 12 months before the claim, with carve-outs for confidentiality, data protection, IP
   infringement and wilful misconduct. The cap must be mutual, not one-sided, and must apply to the aggregate, not per claim. "The greater of fees
   paid or a nominal amount" is not a cap.
2. **Excluded damages**: consequential, indirect and loss-of-profit damages excluded on both sides, and a clear statement that data-loss costs sit
   inside the cap, not outside it.
3. **Termination**: notice periods, whether the customer can terminate for convenience and on what notice, what happens to prepaid fees and to data
   export on termination. Cure period for material breach is 30 days standard; termination for non-payment is immediate.
4. **Payment terms**: net 30 standard, late payment interest, suspension rights after a defined number of days of non-payment, and what counts as a
   disputed invoice.
5. **Exclusivity**: any exclusivity is a concession with a price. Require a named segment, a defined territory, a minimum commitment, and an end date.
   "Non-compete" language that blocks selling to anyone in the industry is an escalation.
6. **IP and licence**: the customer owns their data and grants the licence needed to process it; we keep the platform and all improvements; no
   assignment of our code or IP. No "work made for hire" covering our platform.
7. **Warranty and SLA**: an uptime commitment with a service-credit regime as the sole remedy, and no warranty that the service is uninterrupted or
   error-free.
8. **Governing law and jurisdiction**: our jurisdiction or a neutral one, with the venue named. Avoid a venue where we cannot afford to enforce.
9. **Indemnity**: mutual, limited to third-party claims. Our IP indemnity must not extend to the customer's use of the service in violation of the
   agreement or law.
10. **Confidentiality**: mutual, 3 years post-termination, with trade secrets protected for as long as they qualify.
11. **Assignment and change of control**: neither party may assign without consent, with the standard exception for a sale of substantially all
    assets, and the customer may not assign to a competitor.
12. **Data protection**: reference to the DPA, no consent to processing beyond the agreement, no audit right over source code or infrastructure.
13. **Auto-renewal**: renewal term, notice window, and the price change permitted at renewal (capped at a percentage or the lesser of increase and a
    stated number). A 90-day notice window on our side is a trap.
14. **Acceptable use and sanctions**: acceptable use policy incorporated by reference, no compliance warranty we cannot support.
15. **Boilerplate**: signature, counterparts, entire agreement, severability, notices. Check that the notice address is one actually monitored.

## Step 3 - Ask the counterparty the questions the paper cannot answer

Five questions, in writing, attached to the redline:

1. Which clauses did your counsel actually change, and who approved them?
2. What is the business reason for each change, and is it a template default or a real requirement?
3. What does your procurement, legal or security team require that is not in the document?
4. Which obligations are you asking us to accept for all customers, and which only for your company?
5. What is the deadline, who signs, and is there a security or procurement step between legal sign-off and signature?

Attach the answers to the record. Most objectionable edits turn out to be a template default, and they usually move when labelled as such.

## Step 4 - Escalate, do not accept

Send these to a qualified lawyer or to the founder before signing, even if they look minor:

- Liability uncapped, or the cap excludes the main risk in the deal.
- Liability cap below 12 months of fees, or asymmetric (one party capped, the other not).
- Excluded damages waived on one side only.
- Exclusivity, non-compete, or most-favoured-nation pricing.
- IP assignment beyond the deliverables, or a work-made-for-hire clause over our platform.
- Source code escrow, an audit right over source code, or a no-derivative-works restriction.
- Termination without refund of prepaid fees, or payment terms beyond net 60 without a credit check.
- Auto-renewal with a notice window we cannot meet, or an uncapped price increase at renewal.
- A change-of-control clause that lets the customer walk on a funding round.
- Uncapped indemnity, or indemnity for the customer's own unlawful use.
- Governing law or venue in a jurisdiction where enforcement is impractical.
- A request to sign before procurement is complete, or to sign a document whose final version has not been shown.
- Anything written by the counterparty's counsel that changes who owns the customer relationship.

Escalation means: do not sign, do not accept verbally, write "pending review" and name the person who will decide, with a date.

## What a good answer looks like

- A one-page memo: deal type, leverage, value, term, and the recommendation (sign, sign with edits, escalate).
- A clause table with position, deviation and severity for the 15 clauses.
- A short list of redlines, each with the exact replacement wording.
- The 5 questions to the counterparty, sent.
- The escalation list with an owner and a date for each item.
- Nothing signed before the escalation items are resolved.

## Common traps

- Reading for the commercial terms and skimming the liability section, which is where the money is.
- Accepting "the cap does not apply to confidentiality or data claims" because it appears in every agreement; that carve-out is the whole downside of
  the deal.
- Agreeing to exclusivity in exchange for a discount without pricing the forgone segment.
- Letting a deadline force a signature on unreviewed redlines.
- Marking up a 40-page agreement without classifying it first.
- Confusing a template default with a deliberate counterparty requirement.
- Recording the review in email instead of attaching the final version of the document.
