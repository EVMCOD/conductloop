# Competitive Positioning

A written position for one product: the segment, the alternative they use
today, the differentiated value, and the reason to believe. Ends in a
one-line positioning statement plus the proof that supports it.

## When to use

- Before writing a landing page, a pricing page, or a comparison page.
- When a competitor ships a feature you lack, or you ship one they lack.
- When sales calls stall on "how are you different from X".
- Before naming a competitor in any public copy.

Not for: a formal market map (use opportunity assessment), or picking which
product to build next (use portfolio prioritization).

## Step 1 - Name the real alternatives

For the target segment, list every way the job gets done today, including
the one that is not software:

- Direct competitors: they already do this job.
- Adjacent tools: spreadsheets, chat threads, an agency, a freelancer.
- The status quo: doing nothing, or a manual process that costs hours.

For each alternative record: who it serves, what it costs per month, what it
does badly, and why a buyer stays anyway (switching cost, habit, contract).
Five alternatives is usually enough; if you cannot name five, the market is
thin and the position is mostly about distribution.

## Step 2 - Find the wedge

Look for a dimension where the target segment is underserved. Viable wedges,
in rough order of durability:

1. A segment other competitors ignore (they are too small to bother with).
2. A workflow they handle badly (they target a different use case).
3. A constraint they cannot change (no self-hosting, no local-first, no
   compliance path, no offline mode).
4. A price point they structurally cannot serve (their unit economics break
   below it).
5. A channel they do not sell through.

The wedge must be a reason a buyer would switch, not a feature you have and
they lack. A feature gap is not a position: it is a roadmap item.

## Step 3 - Test the position against the "so what"

For each candidate benefit, complete: "so what, and compared to what?" If the
answer does not name a concrete alternative and a concrete improvement, the
benefit is a feature, not a differentiator. Two passes are the maximum: the
first pass usually produces a feature list, the second produces the position.

## Step 4 - Find the reason to believe

A reason to believe is something true today that makes the claim credible:

- A proprietary dataset or an artifact only you produce.
- A technical constraint you solved that competitors have not.
- A track record: shipped dates, customers, uptime, an audit.
- A distribution channel: an audience, a marketplace listing, a partnership.
- A structural cost advantage: your infra is local, so the marginal cost is
  zero.

If there is no reason to believe, the position is a wish. Write that down as
a finding: it is usually worth more than the positioning work itself.

## Step 5 - Write the statement and check it

Template (one sentence, 12 to 25 words):

> For [specific segment] who [need or situation], [product] is the
> [category] that [benefit]. Unlike [alternative], we [unique value].

Three checks before it ships:

- Substitution test: if a competitor's name replaced yours, would the
  sentence still be true? If yes, it says nothing.
- Evidence test: every noun in it is defensible with something you can show.
- Specificity test: a segment you could name ten real people in. "Small
  businesses" fails; "solo SaaS founders running three to five products"
  passes.

## What a good answer looks like

A one-page brief:

- Segment (narrow, named, with why they are underserved).
- Top three alternatives with price and their real weakness.
- The wedge, in one sentence.
- The differentiated benefit, with the "so what" completed.
- The reason to believe, with the artifact that proves it.
- The positioning statement.
- The two or three claims you will NOT make; the boundaries keep it sharp.
- A "when we lose" note: the situation where the alternative wins.

## Common traps

- Positioning on a feature. Features get copied; positions get believed.
- Comparing against a competitor nobody in the segment uses, or claiming a
  competitor's weakness without evidence.
- Naming yourself against a giant in the first line; it borrows their
  credibility and invites the comparison you cannot win.
- Positioning on price alone, which anchors you as the cheap option and
  attracts buyers who churn on price.
- Writing the statement for an audience the founder finds comfortable instead
  of the one that pays.
- Rewriting the position every month; it should change only when the
  segment, the alternatives, or the evidence changes.
