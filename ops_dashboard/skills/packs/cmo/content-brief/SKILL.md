# Content Brief

The one page that decides what a piece of content says before anyone writes
it. Ends with a brief a writer (human or model) can execute without
guessing.

## When to use

- Before writing a landing page, a comparison page, a guide, or a post.
- When content exists but does not get found, or gets found by the wrong people.
- Before rewriting something that already ranks but does not convert.
- When deciding whether a topic is worth writing at all.

Not for: launch announcements (use a launch plan), or copy inside the
product, which follows the positioning statement instead.

## Step 1 - Define the job and the intent

Answer in one sentence each, before anything else:

- The reader: who specifically is searching, and what are they doing right now
  when they search this?
- The job: what decision or task are they trying to finish? A search is a step
  in a task, not a question to be answered.
- The intent type: informational (learn), commercial (compare), or transactional
  (buy or start). Each needs a different page shape.

Then the query: one primary query, and the 5 to 10 long-tail variations
that mean the same thing. If the primary query has no volume and the
long-tails have intent, target the long-tails and say so in the brief.

## Step 2 - Find the angle

The angle is why this piece exists when 40 others already cover the topic.
Without one, do not write it.

Angles that work for a small portfolio: a number only you have (your own
data, your own test, your own pricing); a workflow for a specific segment
the big articles ignore; an honest comparison, including where the
alternative wins; a tool or template usable in 5 minutes; or something that
changed recently, updated and dated.

Write the angle as one sentence: "This piece shows [reader] how to [task],
which the top results do not do, because [specific asset or angle]".

Test: could a competitor publish this same piece tomorrow? If yes, there is
no angle yet.

## Step 3 - Specify the structure, not the prose

The brief contains the outline, and the writer fills it:

- The title: one sentence, with the primary query's plain wording in it.
- The opening: the answer in the first 100 words. No throat-clearing.
- H2s: one per question the reader actually has, in the order they have it.
- The proof slots: a table or chart, a screenshot, a number, a quote. Name where
  each comes from. A brief without proof slots produces content without proof.
- The internal links: 3 to 5, named, pointing at the pages that convert. If the
  piece has no natural link to the pricing page, it is not for the segment that
  buys.
- The call to action: one, matching the reader's intent. Informational readers
  get a newsletter or a tool, not a sales call.

## Step 4 - Make it quotable by an answer engine

Answer engines quote the passage that answers the question directly. To be
quoted, the piece needs:

- A direct answer sentence right under each H2, in under 40 words, with no
  preamble. "X costs Y, and here is when Z."
- Explicit entities: name the tools, standards, methods and people. Vague
  phrasing loses to a page that names them.
- A date on anything that changes ("as of 2026", "updated quarterly").
- A comparison table. Tables get extracted and cited; prose does not.
- Question-shaped H2s that match how people ask, not how the company organises
  its features.
- Numbers with units and a source: "8 seconds versus 24 seconds, measured on 500
  records" beats "3x faster".

## Step 5 - Set the measurement

- The target query and its ranking page, checked 30 days after publishing.
- The conversion: what the reader should do, and how it is tracked.
- The refresh date. A piece with a number in it goes stale in 6 months, so put
  the refresh date in the brief, not in a mental note.

## What a good answer looks like

One page: the reader, job, intent type, primary query and long-tails; the
angle in one sentence, and why the existing results do not cover it; the
outline (title, opening, each H2 with its answer sentence, the proof slots
with their sources, the internal links by name, the call to action); the
quotability checklist (direct answers, named entities, date, table, units,
sources); the measurement (query, target page, conversion, refresh date);
and a "do not write this if" line naming the condition that would make the
piece not worth it.

## Common traps

- Writing before the angle is settled, so the piece says what everyone says.
- Targeting a high-volume query with commercial intent the page cannot serve.
- Structuring by internal features instead of by the reader's questions.
- A brief with no proof slots, so the draft invents numbers to fill them.
- Forgetting the internal links, and writing a page that helps nobody buy.
- Treating a piece as evergreen when it contains a number and a price.
- Writing for a keyword instead of for a person with a job to finish.
