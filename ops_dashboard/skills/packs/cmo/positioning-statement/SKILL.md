# Positioning Statement

The internal sentence every piece of copy derives from. Ends with one
sentence, plus the claims the product will and will not make.

## When to use

- Before a landing page, a pitch, or a campaign.
- When copy is rewritten and still does not convert.
- When a competitor takes a message that used to be distinctive.
- When deciding what the product is "for" (and what it is not for).

Not for: deciding what to build (use competitive positioning for the market
view, portfolio prioritization for the choice), or a content outline.

## Step 1 - Find the segment

Not a market, not an audience, a segment: a group of people in the same
situation, with the same job, who can be named. Write it as "[who they are]
who [situation they are in]".

Test it: name 10 real people who fit, and 10 who almost fit but do not. If
you cannot, the segment is too broad, and the statement will say something
true of everybody, which is the same as saying nothing about anybody. The
narrower the segment, the easier the copy and the more obvious the buyers.

## Step 2 - Name the alternative honestly

The alternative is what the segment does today when they do not use the
product: a competitor with a specific name, a category of tool
("spreadsheets", "agency", "internal tools"), a person they hire, a manual
process, or doing nothing.

Name the one that wins most often. If the honest answer is "they do
nothing", that is the alternative, and the positioning has to make switching
worth it rather than assume it.

Ask: why has nobody switched away from the alternative yet? That reason is
the real obstacle and belongs in the strategy, not just the copy.

## Step 3 - State the unique value

One benefit, stated as a change in the reader's situation, not as a feature.
Bad: "Real-time sync with 40+ integrations." Bad: "The fastest way to manage
your portfolio." Good: "Know which of your products is losing money before
the month closes."

Test: swap in a competitor's name. If the sentence stays true, it is not
unique. If it becomes false, it is positioning.

Then the reason to believe: the fact that makes it credible today. Data
only you have, a constraint you solved, a track record, a channel, a cost
advantage. If there is no reason to believe, the statement is a wish, and
that finding is worth more than the copy.

## Step 4 - Name the category

The category sets the expectations the reader brings, and it decides who
compares you to whom. A category you create ("the operating dashboard for a
micro-portfolio") is powerful but expensive: you have to teach it. A
category you enter (a comparison with the incumbent) is cheap but caps you
at the incumbent's expectations. A hybrid is usually right for a small
product: a familiar frame with one new word.

Rule: pick the category a customer would use when telling a colleague what
the product is, not the category a competitor's homepage uses.

## Step 5 - Write the statement and the boundaries

The format, one sentence of 12 to 25 words.

> For [segment] who [situation], [product] is the [category] that
> [benefit]. Unlike [alternative], we [unique value + reason to believe].

Then the boundaries, which most statements skip and which are what make a
position defensible: the claims we will NOT make (2 or 3), who this is not
for, and what we lose to the alternative and when. A statement without
boundaries drifts, and every new page pulls it in a different direction.

## Step 6 - Turn it into assets

One statement produces a small set of reusable assets, and nothing else
does: the one-line version (headers and bios); the 3 message pillars, each
with one proof point (pages and posts); the 5 objection answers, drawn from
the segment's real objections (support and sales); and the comparison
framing, what the alternative is good at and where it stops (the comparison
page). Every asset must trace back to the statement. If a page or post
cannot be traced to it, it is off-message: cut it or change the statement.

## What a good answer looks like

One page: the segment with 10 example names and the near-misses; the
alternative and why people stay with it; the unique value in one sentence
and the substitution test result; the reason to believe with its artifact;
the category and why that frame was chosen; the positioning statement; the
claims not made and who it is not for; and the derived assets (one line, 3
pillars with proof, 5 objections).

## Common traps

- Positioning for "businesses" or "teams", which describes nobody.
- Naming a feature instead of a benefit.
- Writing a claim a competitor could also make, and calling it unique.
- Skipping the reason to believe, so the copy sounds like advertising.
- No boundaries, so the position drifts with every new page.
- Writing the statement and never using it, then wondering why the copy
  feels scattered.
- Claiming to be for everyone, and being remembered by nobody.
